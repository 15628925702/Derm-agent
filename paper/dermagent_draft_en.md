# DermAgent: A Structured Agent Reasoning Framework Under a Fixed Dermatology Vision-Language Backbone

Abstract

A central challenge in multimodal dermatology diagnosis is that backbone vision-language models can often produce an initial judgment, yet much of the diagnostic process remains implicit, making it difficult to explicitly carry out clinically important actions such as comparison, exclusion, uncertainty assessment, information-gap detection, and experience accumulation. As a result, these systems are difficult to audit, difficult to analyze when errors occur, and difficult to optimize at the peripheral-strategy level under frozen evaluation conditions. We present DermAgent, a structured agent reasoning framework for dermatology whose goal is not to replace the backbone model, but to provide a structured evidence scaffold before final diagnosis while keeping Qwen as the sole final diagnostician.

DermAgent organizes diagnosis into initial perception, layered experience retrieval, skill retrieval and selection, skill execution, evidence organization, final diagnosis, and reflection with write-back. In contrast to approaches that turn auxiliary modules into disease classifiers or voting components, DermAgent defines skills as structured clinical reasoning actions that produce structured evidence rather than final disease labels. Its layered experience bank is not a standard similar-case retriever, but a three-level experience system composed of raw case memory, tactical experience, and abstract experience, jointly influencing planning, skill use, and evidence routing. In addition, the cognition state, layered experience bank, and parameterized policy layer are explicitly separated to support cross-case strategy memory, experience accumulation, and auditable version control. Based on these states, the system organizes skill outputs, retrieved experience, risk cues, and uncertainty signals into an evidence package before final diagnosis. Qwen remains the sole final diagnostician, and the agent does not replace the backbone.

At the implementation level, the current codebase already realizes the main execution path, state objects, the skill bank, experience write-back, cognition updates, trainable peripheral policy components, and a frozen evaluation protocol. The system supports separate training or versioning of the planner/controller, retrieval reranker, evidence calibration module, and policy layer, while keeping backbone weights fixed. To avoid contamination, formal evaluation is designed to be frozen and fair: split states are fixed, case lists are fixed, online write-back is disabled, and direct baselines and agent variants are compared under the same protocol and same-backbone comparison setting.

The current project remains at a preliminary validation stage. Early controlled comparisons and analysis scripts are already implemented, but complete frozen results, finalized comparative numbers, and broader external validation are still under ongoing validation. Accordingly, this paper emphasizes problem formulation, method design, implementation boundaries, and evaluation discipline, rather than making conclusive performance claims.

Keywords: dermatology diagnosis; medical multimodal models; agent reasoning; structured evidence; skill bank; layered experience bank; cognition state; frozen and fair evaluation

## 1 Introduction

Dermatology diagnosis depends heavily on visual morphology, clinical context, and differential reasoning. Even when a single lesion image provides strong visual cues, real diagnosis is rarely a one-shot image classification problem. Instead, it typically unfolds through a sequence of questions: What is visible? Which differential diagnoses should remain open? Which candidates should be ruled out? What critical information is still missing? Are the current risk signals sufficient to justify escalation or further examination? For melanocytic lesions, keratinocytic lesions, and several high-confusion disease groups, local morphological similarity, incomplete metadata, missing temporal information, and mismatches between risk cues and superficial visual appearance often make the reasoning process inherently multi-stage and constraint-driven. Dermatology therefore requires not only visual recognition, but also structured clinical reasoning and auditability.

Recent advances in general-purpose vision-language models and medical multimodal foundation models have created new opportunities for dermatology AI. These models can jointly process images, text, and structured clinical metadata under a unified interface, and can produce lesion descriptions, differential candidates, and explanatory text. Compared with conventional disease-specific classifiers, they are closer to an open-ended clinical reasoning paradigm and offer a stronger foundation for general medical AI systems. However, several key limitations remain. First, their reasoning is mostly implicit, making it difficult to determine whether the model actually performed clinically important actions such as comparison, exclusion, uncertainty assessment, or information-gap detection. Second, when facing high-confusion cases, high-risk cases, or information-poor cases, one-shot generation often struggles to distinguish between “the evidence is insufficient” and “the conclusion is already warranted.” Third, directly querying a backbone model usually does not yield a persistent cross-case experience structure, nor does it naturally transform past errors, confusion patterns, or behavioral preferences into trainable and auditable peripheral capabilities.

One natural response is to improve prompting or adopt prompt chaining, for example by asking the model to first describe the lesion, then produce a differential diagnosis, and finally output a conclusion. While such approaches are simple to implement and can be useful in some settings, their limitations are equally clear. Prompting generally controls output format or shallow procedural steps, but does not explicitly model which reasoning actions should be triggered, which past experiences should be retrieved, or which evidence should enter the final diagnostic context. More importantly, prompting does not naturally support persistent state. Helpful skills, ineffective skills, recurring confusion pairs, and high-value experience fragments from one case are difficult to reuse in a structured way in later cases. As a result, the system still behaves more like one-shot question answering than a framework that can adapt strategy, organize evidence, and evolve across cases.

This motivates the need for structured agent reasoning. In dermatology, the more appropriate direction is not to let peripheral modules produce final disease conclusions, but to externalize clinically meaningful local reasoning actions and let them contribute to the construction of pre-diagnostic evidence. Following this view, skills can be defined as atomic clinical reasoning actions, such as morphology analysis, color-pattern analysis, border and surface examination, differential comparison, exclusion reasoning, malignancy risk assessment, uncertainty assessment, and information-gap detection. Experience should not be reduced to a similar-case cache, but should instead distinguish concrete case memory, tactical experience, and cross-case abstract experience. On top of this, the system requires a cross-case cognition state that records confusion patterns, skill helpfulness statistics, retrieval preferences, and strategic biases, so that skill selection, experience selection, and evidence organization become evolvable peripheral policy rather than static templates. Such a structure makes implicit clinical actions explicit and, at the same time, creates interfaces for subsequent training, auditing, and error analysis.

However, once agent reasoning is introduced, the question of system boundary becomes equally important. If peripheral modules are allowed to act as independent diagnosticians whose outputs are later voted on or aggregated, the system quickly degenerates into a multi-module voting diagnosis framework. If skills are implemented as rigid heuristic classifiers, structured reasoning collapses into a collection of attached rule-based classifiers. Both directions may appear to enhance the system, but both blur the locus of final responsibility and make behavior harder to interpret and compare fairly. In medical settings, if the agent and the baseline differ simultaneously in backbone, case list, online memory state, or evaluation protocol, any apparent agent gain becomes difficult to attribute. For this reason, we deliberately avoid three directions: multi-module voting diagnosis, rigid heuristic classifier attachments, and non-frozen or mismatched agent-versus-baseline comparisons. Instead, we constrain the agent to act as a structured evidence enhancement layer while leaving final diagnostic responsibility to the underlying vision-language model.

Motivated by these considerations, we introduce DermAgent, a structured agent reasoning framework for dermatology diagnosis. Its central design choice is to keep the underlying vision-language model unchanged while assigning the agent responsibility for experience-driven skill organization, experience retrieval, evidence routing, reflection/write-back, and cognition updates, and leaving both initial perception and final diagnosis to the backbone. In other words, DermAgent does not replace the backbone and does not attempt to overturn the diagnosis through peripheral overrides. Instead, it influences the final diagnostic context by constructing a more auditable and clinically meaningful evidence package. Within this framework, skill modules produce structured evidence rather than final disease labels; the layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer are explicitly separated; planner/controller, retrieval reranker, and evidence calibration are implemented as trainable or versioned peripheral strategy components; and formal evaluation is designed as frozen and fair so that structured agent reasoning can be compared against direct prompting under the same backbone, same case list, and same protocol.

This leads to the central research question of the paper: under a fixed vision-language backbone, can structured, experience-driven agent reasoning outperform direct prompting? This is not merely a question of whether adding more modules helps. Rather, it asks whether organizing diagnosis through a skill bank, a layered experience bank, and a cognition state that affects later skill use and evidence routing can provide a stable and fairly evaluable improvement to final diagnosis. Since the current project remains under ongoing validation, our emphasis is on problem formulation, method design, implementation boundaries, and evaluation discipline, rather than on premature claims of conclusive performance gains.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

1. We present DermAgent, a structured agent reasoning framework for dermatology diagnosis that organizes a skill bank, layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer as an auditable peripheral reasoning layer without changing the final diagnostic role of the underlying vision-language model.
2. We design and implement a dual structure of a skill bank and a layered experience bank, in which skills are defined as atomic clinical reasoning actions, while experience is organized into raw case memory, tactical experience, and abstract experience, jointly supporting skill selection, experience selection, and evidence organization.
3. We introduce an explicit cognition state and reflection/write-back mechanism, enabling the system to record confusion patterns, skill helpfulness, and strategic preferences across cases, thereby providing a structural basis for controlled agent evolution while preserving the separation between experience, cognition, and policy.
4. We implement the planner/controller, retrieval reranker, evidence calibration, and policy versioning as trainable or versioned peripheral components, and explicitly constrain them to affect only the production and organization of structured evidence rather than replace the backbone’s final diagnosis.
5. We establish a frozen and fair evaluation protocol for agent-versus-baseline comparison, emphasizing same-backbone comparison, matched case lists, matched protocols, and disabled online write-back to reduce unfair comparison and state contamination.

## 2 Related Work

### 2.1 Dermatology vision-language models and reasoning models

Recent work on dermatology vision-language models and domain-specialized multimodal models has become increasingly important in medical AI. [Citation Placeholder: Skin-R1, SkinGPT-R1, SkinGPT-4, related dermatology VLM papers] One line of work adapts general multimodal foundation models to dermatology tasks by combining lesion images, clinical text, and structured metadata to improve open-ended diagnosis, differential generation, and medical description. Another line emphasizes dermatology-specific specialization through data construction, instruction tuning, or reasoning-oriented adaptation, so that the model becomes more consistent in dermatology terminology, lesion representation, and diagnostic question answering. These efforts jointly suggest that dermatology should not be treated as a simple closed-set image classification problem, and that diagnostic outputs increasingly include reasoning traces or differential diagnoses rather than single labels.

However, these approaches still focus primarily on improving the backbone itself, including model weights, domain knowledge injection, reasoning style shaping, or multimodal alignment. Even when the model can produce richer reasoning text, key diagnostic actions usually remain implicit: whether the model truly carried out exclusion reasoning, whether it explicitly recognized evidence conflicts, which past experiences may help the current case, and which evidence should be retained before final decision are often not independently controllable, trainable, or auditable. For the question studied here, making the backbone “reason better” is not sufficient to answer whether structured peripheral reasoning can help under a fixed backbone.

DermAgent differs from this line of work in a clear way. Its main goal is not to train a new dermatology backbone, nor to replace existing multimodal models with a more heavily specialized one. Instead, we treat the backbone as fixed and as the sole final diagnostician, and shift the methodological emphasis to the structured evidence enhancement layer before final diagnosis: how skills are organized, how experience is retrieved and abstracted, how the cognition state influences later strategy, and how evidence is calibrated and packaged for final diagnosis. DermAgent is therefore better understood as a reasoning exoskeleton around a dermatology backbone rather than a re-trained backbone model.

### 2.2 Medical agents and tool-augmented reasoning

With the rise of large-model agent frameworks, medical AI has also seen increasing interest in tool use, workflow decomposition, and multi-step reasoning. [Citation Placeholder: medical agents, tool-augmented reasoning in medicine] These systems often rely on retrievers, knowledge bases, rule-based tools, external modules, or multi-step planning to move beyond single-turn generation. Compared with direct prompting, a major contribution of medical agents is that they externalize intermediate steps that would otherwise remain hidden inside a single model call, thereby allowing the system to access outside resources and, to some extent, become more interpretable.

That said, many current medical agents remain focused on text question answering, literature retrieval, report generation, or general clinical consultation. Even when medical images are involved, the agent structure often centers on calling several tools to complete a multi-step task rather than constructing a long-horizon, evolvable, and fairly evaluable reasoning scaffold for a specific high-risk diagnostic setting. In dermatology, the value of tools is not merely to add an external information source, but to organize clinically meaningful local actions into a visual-diagnosis-centered evidence production process, while keeping that evidence structured, auditable, and reusable before final diagnosis.

DermAgent differs from general medical agents in three main respects. First, it is not designed as a purely text-based medical QA assistant, but explicitly targets multimodal dermatology diagnosis. Second, its “tools” are not arbitrary APIs, but modules constrained to be structured clinical reasoning actions with clear clinical semantics. Third, DermAgent does not make the agent a new final decision maker; instead, the backbone retains final diagnosis, while the agent organizes evidence, experience, and strategy. This design sets it apart from medical agent frameworks that aggregate multiple tools directly into a final answer.

### 2.3 Skill- and experience-based agent learning

Several recent studies have explored separating agent capability into skill and experience objects. [Citation Placeholder: XSKILL, TARSE, MemSkill, MACRO] XSKILL and TARSE emphasize that skills and experience should be explicitly distinguished: skills are closer to reusable action capabilities or procedural templates, while experience captures task-instance-specific history, constraints, and feedback. This separation avoids collapsing all historical trajectories into a single memory pool and can improve controller precision when choosing what to invoke in a new context. MACRO and related work emphasize discovering reusable multi-step procedures from prior execution, while MemSkill links memory, skills, controller behavior, and skill evolution so that memory affects not only retrieval but also capability growth.

These works suggest that a more capable agent should not rely solely on one-shot prompting, but should instead have composable capability units, reusable experience carriers, and explicit control mechanisms. However, most existing work of this kind is developed in general agent settings, software tasks, text interaction, or abstract decision environments. Their core objects are not dermatology image diagnosis, and they do not need to maintain a strict boundary around final medical conclusions. In such settings, a “skill” can simply be a tool that completes a subtask, and “experience” can often be represented as scripts, programs, or operational traces. In medical diagnosis, by contrast, skills can easily degenerate into peripheral disease classifiers, and experience can collapse into simple nearest-case retrieval unless their semantic boundaries are carefully controlled.

DermAgent occupies an intersection with these lines of work while imposing stricter boundaries suitable for medical diagnosis. It adopts the intuition of a dual skill-and-experience structure, but constrains skills to structured clinical reasoning actions, extends experience into raw, tactical, and abstract layers, and limits the controller to skill selection, experience selection, and evidence routing rather than direct diagnosis. In this sense, DermAgent borrows the organizational principles of skill-based and experience-based agent learning without directly importing them as generic agent components.

### 2.4 Evolving cognition / memory / self-improving agents

Another relevant line of research studies how agents can evolve over time through memory, reflection, self-improvement signals, and policy adaptation. [Citation Placeholder: AutoAgent and related evolving-memory agents] AutoAgent, for example, highlights the role of evolving cognition and elastic memory, arguing that an improving agent cannot rely solely on a static prompt but must maintain cross-episode state about its capabilities, preferences, failure modes, and feedback.

While these works provide important inspiration for long-horizon agent improvement, self-improvement in medical diagnosis cannot be treated as unconstrained online learning. Medical settings require a stricter separation of which states represent experience content, which represent strategic preferences, and which represent trainable policy. They also require careful treatment of whether online write-back contaminates formal evaluation, whether reflection updates cause uncontrolled drift, and whether long-horizon changes undermine fairness against the baseline. In this setting, evolving cognition is not simply “remembering more,” but building explicit boundaries among memory, cognition, strategy, and evaluation discipline.

DermAgent is positioned as a framework with the structural conditions for controlled evolution, rather than as a fully validated self-improving system. More concretely, it explicitly separates the layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer; uses reflection/write-back to convert case-level execution into layered experience; uses the cognition state to record confusion patterns, skill helpfulness, failure statistics, and retrieval preferences; and allows planner, retrieval reranking, and evidence organization to exploit these states in later cases. At the same time, online write-back is disabled during formal evaluation, and split-specific frozen state snapshots are enforced so that system evolution is not confused with unfair experimentation.

### 2.5 Positioning of DermAgent

Taken together, existing work has advanced medical multimodal models, tool-augmented agents, skill/experience separation, and evolving cognition from different angles, but their primary concerns do not fully overlap. Dermatology VLM work primarily strengthens the backbone; medical agents emphasize multi-step task execution and tool coordination; skill- and experience-based agent learning offers an abstraction for organizing capability and control; and self-improving agent work emphasizes long-horizon memory and cognitive adaptation. DermAgent lies at the intersection of these trends, but its research question is more specific: under a fixed backbone, can one build a structured exoskeleton composed of a skill bank, layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer for dermatology image diagnosis, and evaluate it under a frozen and fair same-backbone comparison protocol?

DermAgent is therefore distinct from dermatology-specialized backbone training, from purely text-based medical agents, and from voting-style systems that aggregate multiple peripheral diagnosticians into a final decision. Its role is to make local clinical actions, layered experience, cross-case cognition, and evidence packaging explicit while leaving final diagnostic authority untouched.

## 3 Method

### 3.1 Problem Formulation

We study the problem of structured reasoning enhancement for multimodal dermatology diagnosis. Given a skin lesion image and optional clinical metadata, such as age, anatomical site, lesion diameter, symptom changes, and partial history, the system is expected to produce a final diagnosis and associated explanatory information. Unlike a setting in which one simply sends the input to a vision-language model and requests a direct answer, we ask whether, under a fixed vision-language backbone, a structured, experience-driven agent layer can provide a more auditable, more trainable, and more fairly comparable evidence enhancement process before final diagnosis.

In this setting, the backbone is not the object being replaced or retrained. It is treated as fixed and as the sole final diagnostician. DermAgent is not intended to let peripheral modules compete for final decision authority; instead, it externalizes local clinical actions, experience retrieval, and evidence integration that would otherwise remain implicit inside one-shot generation. Formally, the system can be written as

\[
y = F_{\text{backbone}}(x, m, \mathcal{E}(x,m;\theta_{\text{agent}})),
\]

where \(x\) denotes the lesion image, \(m\) denotes optional metadata, \(F_{\text{backbone}}\) denotes the fixed vision-language backbone, \(\mathcal{E}\) denotes the structured evidence package produced by the agent, and \(\theta_{\text{agent}}\) corresponds to peripheral strategy parameters such as planning, retrieval scoring, and evidence calibration. The main question of the paper is therefore not how to build a new diagnostic backbone, but whether structured agent reasoning can outperform direct prompting under a fixed backbone.

### 3.2 System Overview

DermAgent is a structured reasoning exoskeleton for dermatology diagnosis. It decomposes the diagnostic process into two backbone-centered stages and one agent-centered intermediate stage. First, Qwen performs initial visual understanding over the image and metadata, producing a lesion summary, initial differential candidates, and preliminary uncertainty cues. Second, the agent uses this initial perception to carry out experience retrieval, skill selection, skill execution, and evidence organization. Third, Qwen receives the resulting evidence package and generates the final diagnosis. Thus, the backbone is called twice in the overall workflow: once for initial visual understanding and candidate generation, and once for final diagnosis generation. The final answer is always produced by Qwen.

The intermediate agent layer has four main responsibilities. First, it retrieves layered prior experience relevant to the current case, including raw case memory, tactical experience, and abstract experience. Second, it selects appropriate structured clinical reasoning actions based on initial perception, metadata, retrieved experience, and the cognition state. Third, it organizes skill outputs, experience cues, risk flags, and uncertainty signals into a structured evidence package. Fourth, when the run mode allows it, it performs reflection and write-back after diagnosis and updates the cross-case cognition state. In this sense, DermAgent does not alter final diagnostic authority; it enhances the final context through an explicit and auditable intermediate reasoning structure.

### 3.3 Design Principles

The design of DermAgent follows five principles. First, the single-final-diagnostician principle: Qwen remains the sole final diagnostician, and the agent neither outputs final disease labels nor overrides the backbone through voting or peripheral decision modules. Second, the structured-evidence principle: the agent is designed to produce, filter, and organize structured evidence, not to produce a parallel diagnosis. Third, the layered-state principle: the layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer represent distinct levels of state, corresponding to knowledge content, strategic memory and behavioral preferences, and trainable control parameters, respectively. Fourth, the frozen-backbone principle: trainable components such as the controller, retrieval scoring, and evidence calibration can be optimized independently, but the backbone weights remain fixed and final diagnostic authority is unchanged. Fifth, the fairness principle: gains from the agent must be evaluated under frozen state, matched case lists, and same-backbone comparison to reduce state drift and unfair experimental variation.

Together, these principles define DermAgent as a structured, experience-driven reasoning layer rather than a voting system, a collection of heuristic classifiers, or simply a different backbone model.

### 3.4 Inference Pipeline

At inference time, DermAgent follows a multi-stage pipeline. Given the input image and optional metadata, Qwen first performs initial visual understanding and produces lesion descriptions, initial differential candidates, and uncertainty cues. Based on this output, the system retrieves historical experience from the layered experience bank and retrieves skill candidates from the skill bank. The planner/controller then selects which skills to run by combining initial perception, clinical metadata, retrieved experience, skill retrieval signals, and preferences and statistics stored in the cognition state.

The selected skills generate structured evidence for the current case, including observational cues, comparison cues, risk cues, uncertainty descriptions, and information-gap signals. The agent then reorganizes these outputs together with retrieved experience into an evidence package used for final diagnosis. This package is not itself a diagnosis. Rather, it is a structured context interface that contains organized summaries of initial perception, layered experience, skill outputs, risk flags, uncertainty signals, and explanatory notes. Finally, Qwen reads both the original input and the evidence package to produce the final diagnosis. When write-back is enabled, the system then performs reflection and extracts case outcomes, skill helpfulness, experience candidates, and cognition updates. Under formal evaluation, this write-back step does not update online state.

### 3.5 Skill Bank

In DermAgent, a skill is defined as an atomic clinical reasoning action. An action here does not mean that the module independently completes diagnosis. Rather, it denotes a local reasoning responsibility with a clear clinical semantic boundary, such as explicitly extracting lesion morphology, comparing differential candidates, checking consistency between metadata and visual cues, surfacing potential risk signals, or identifying uncertainty and information gaps. Accordingly, the basic unit of the skill bank is not a disease predictor, but an evidence producer: each skill generates structured evidence for downstream evidence organization and final diagnosis.

This design differs fundamentally from turning peripheral modules into disease classifiers. First, skills in DermAgent do not output final disease labels and do not assume final diagnostic responsibility. Because Qwen remains the sole final diagnostician, the role of a skill is to supplement and organize clinical actions that the backbone may not explicitly perform in a single generation, rather than compete for final decision authority. Second, defining skills as structured clinical reasoning actions prevents them from collapsing into rigid sub-classifiers. If each skill were trained to predict a disease class or label subset, the system would quickly become a collection of attached classifiers plus an aggregator, undermining responsibility boundaries and making module semantics and error sources harder to audit. By constraining skill outputs to structured evidence rather than final labels, DermAgent makes comparison, exclusion, risk review, and uncertainty handling explicit without creating another layer of final diagnosticians.

From a methodological standpoint, this definition improves interpretability, auditability, and trainability. Since each skill corresponds to a relatively stable clinical action, the planner/controller can reason explicitly about which actions are needed for a case instead of implicitly combining sub-classifiers. Reflection can evaluate each skill in terms of helpfulness, redundancy, or possible harm. In addition, the trigger conditions, outputs, and eventual contributions of each skill to the evidence package can be tracked in execution records. The skill bank is therefore not just a collection of static functions, but a selectable, analyzable, and versioned library of reasoning actions.

In the current implementation, the skill bank covers several evidence-producing categories relevant to dermatology. These include perception-oriented evidence extraction, which refines initial visual understanding into more explicit morphological, color, border/surface, distributional, and lesion-description evidence; differential comparison and related exclusion-oriented skills; metadata consistency checking; malignancy risk assessment; specialist confusion auditing around high-confusion diagnostic pairs; and uncertainty assessment. The current system also includes contradiction checking, information-gap detection, and escalation recommendation modules related to uncertainty handling and safety boundaries. These modules are already integrated into the existing execution path, although their full benefit remains under ongoing validation.

Although these skill types differ functionally, they share one common methodological constraint: they output structured evidence rather than final disease labels. Even specialist-oriented skills focused on high-confusion pairs are not designed to adjudicate the disease directly. As part of the system, the skill bank further serves three roles: it is maintained as versioned source-level assets; it is dynamically selected by the planner/controller based on case state, retrieved experience, and the cognition state; and its outputs enter the evidence package rather than vote directly on the answer.

### 3.6 Layered Experience Bank

The layered experience bank in DermAgent is not a standard nearest-neighbor case retrieval system, nor a memory pool that simply caches historical cases without internal structure. Its core premise is that dermatology diagnosis requires not only remembering which images looked similar, but also remembering which reasoning actions were helpful in which contexts, which confusion patterns deserve attention, and which recurring cross-case regularities can be abstracted into reusable evidence biases. Accordingly, experience is organized as a layered structure rather than compressed into a single similarity space.

In the current implementation, the layered experience bank has three principal levels. The first is raw case memory, which preserves case-level experience content, including inputs, past outputs, correctness-related signals, and reflection-derived information. This level addresses instance fidelity: when the system encounters a case that resembles a prior one or lies in the same confusion cluster, raw case memory provides the closest reference to the original example. The second is tactical experience, which does not focus on whole-case similarity but on local condition-action-outcome relations, such as which skill combinations help under certain uncertainty conditions, which risk cues should be front-loaded, or which comparison actions are especially useful in specific situations. This level captures reusable local reasoning strategy. The third is abstract experience, which lifts recurring patterns across cases into more abstract prototypes, rule candidates, or confusion memories, and thus captures cross-case knowledge that is transferable beyond individual examples.

The key consequence of this design is that experience influences much more than retrieval. Experience naturally affects retrieval by providing case-specific, tactical, and abstract context. It also affects planning, because tactical or abstract experiences can suggest that the current case resembles a high-confusion, high-risk, or information-poor pattern, thereby shifting which skills are prioritized. Experience further affects skill use itself, because skills may explicitly reference learning points, confusion cues, or risk cues from past experience. Finally, experience enters evidence organization and influences which historical evidence is retained, highlighted, or downweighted in the final evidence package. The layered experience bank is therefore a content layer that spans retrieval, planning, execution, and evidence organization, rather than an auxiliary similar-case retriever.

### 3.7 Cognition State

If the layered experience bank stores “what the system knows,” the cognition state stores “how the system tends to act.” DermAgent defines the cognition state as a cross-case state of strategic memory and behavioral preference rather than a simple cache or count table. Its role is not to replace the experience layer, but to record the preferences, failure patterns, and control priors that emerge through repeated execution, and to feed them back into later skill use, retrieval preference, and evidence routing.

In the current implementation, the cognition state includes several cross-case strategic variables, such as known confusion patterns, preferred skills, retrieval preferences, failure statistics, and skill-level helpful/harmful statistics. Unlike raw case memory or abstract experience, these variables do not attempt to describe what happened in a specific case. Instead, they summarize behavioral regularities across prior runs, such as whether a skill tends to help in a particular type of case, whether a confusion pair repeatedly appears, or whether a retrieval preference should continue to be favored.

The distinction between the layered experience bank and the cognition state is therefore explicit. The experience layer is primarily knowledge content: its basic units are cases, tactical reasoning traces, and cross-case abstract patterns. The cognition state is primarily strategic state: its basic units are preferences, statistics, habits, and control priors. The former tells the system which content from history may be relevant; the latter tells the system which strategic tendencies may be more appropriate in similar situations. In DermAgent, the planner reads preferences and statistics from the cognition state to affect skill selection, and retrieval components can likewise use confusion priors and preference signals from the cognition state when ranking candidates.

This separation matters because it allows cross-case evolution without conflating content updates with strategy updates. Without a distinct cognition state, all long-horizon changes would need to be written into the experience layer, blurring the difference between growing knowledge content and shifting behavioral preference. By representing system-level adaptation explicitly as a separate state object, DermAgent makes such adaptation controllable, auditable, and freezeable during formal evaluation.

### 3.8 Parameterized Policy Layer

While the layered experience bank and the cognition state provide knowledge content and strategic state, they do not directly determine behavior. The component that turns them into concrete control decisions is the parameterized policy layer. Its role is not to modify Qwen’s weights or alter the backbone’s final diagnostic responsibility, but to manage peripheral intermediate strategies in a parameterized, learnable, versioned, and rollback-capable way under a frozen backbone.

In the current system, the parameterized policy layer includes four main parts. The first is the planner policy, which controls skill selection behavior, including thresholds, sparsification strategies, enabled and disabled skills, and the combination of heuristic structure with learned controller behavior. The second is the retrieval policy, which governs candidate ranges, reranking behavior, and other controls for both experience retrieval and skill retrieval. The third is the evidence policy, which controls evidence calibration and evidence organization, including ranking, quotas, trimming, and retention priorities for different evidence types. The fourth is the evaluation gate, which imposes conservative comparison and rollback constraints between candidate and stable policies.

These policy components matter because they make peripheral optimization explicit. In the current implementation, planner/controller behavior, retrieval scoring or reranking, and evidence calibration can all be versioned through policy configuration, checkpoints, and evaluation records. Candidate updates can be compared under frozen protocols and rolled back if needed. In this way, DermAgent optimizes peripheral strategy rather than performing backbone finetuning. It can learn better skill selection, better experience routing, and more stable evidence organization while preserving the backbone’s weights and final diagnostic role.

Together, the layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer form DermAgent’s cognitive-evolution loop. The layered experience bank accumulates case knowledge, tactical patterns, and abstract regularities; the cognition state accumulates cross-case strategic statistics and behavioral preferences; and the parameterized policy layer converts these into executable control decisions in the current case. After each case, reflection and write-back add new outcomes, skill helpfulness information, and experience candidates back into experience and cognition; during training or versioning, the policy layer can update conservatively based on these execution records.

### 3.9 Evidence Package

The endpoint of the intermediate agent layer in DermAgent is not another diagnosis, but a structured evidence package. This object is the main enhanced context visible to the backbone before final diagnosis, and it is one of the key interfaces through which system boundaries are enforced. In other words, DermAgent is not designed so that multiple peripheral modules each produce disease judgments and then aggregate them. Instead, skill outputs, retrieved experience, risk signals, uncertainty cues, and planning rationale are organized into an auditable evidence package before final diagnosis, and Qwen generates the final answer on that basis.

Using the evidence package as a unified interface serves three purposes. First, it constrains the agent’s role to evidence production and evidence organization, thereby structurally preventing peripheral components from competing with the backbone for final diagnostic authority. Second, it improves interpretability and auditability, because the system can record which skills were triggered, which experiences were referenced, which risk or uncertainty signals were front-loaded, and how these elements entered the diagnostic context. Third, it provides a natural interface for training and strategy optimization. Since what enters the final diagnosis stage is not an unstructured intermediate text blob but a grouped and source-aware evidence package, contributions from the planner, retrieval, and evidence calibration modules can be attributed more precisely.

In the current implementation, the evidence package consists of several complementary information blocks, including summaries of initial visual understanding, summaries of raw/tactical/abstract retrieved experience, skill outputs, risk flags, uncertainty signals, contradiction and information-gap indicators, escalation-related signals, and planner rationale. The important point is not the number of fields, but their organization: DermAgent does not simply concatenate all intermediate outputs. Instead, evidence organization and evidence calibration select, group, rank, trim, and quota different evidence sources. Which evidence eventually enters the context, in what order, and with what relative emphasis is therefore itself a modeled peripheral strategy problem.

### 3.10 Reflection and Write-back

If the evidence package corresponds to evidence aggregation in forward reasoning, reflection and write-back correspond to experience consolidation in backward updating. DermAgent does not treat a single case run as an isolated event, but as a source of future experience. After each case reaches final diagnosis, the system produces a structured reflection that summarizes the case-level reasoning process and, when the run mode allows, writes reusable components back into the layered experience bank and cognition state.

In the current implementation, reflection contains not only a reasoning summary, but also a structured case outcome, per-skill helpfulness assessments, potential error or confusion detection, and raw/tactical/abstract experience candidates suitable for future write-back. Write-back is then the controlled state update mechanism built on top of this reflection. For the layered experience bank, write-back transforms case-level results into increments of raw case memory, tactical experience, and abstract experience. For the cognition state, it updates confusion patterns, failure statistics, skill helpful/harmful statistics, and preference information. As a result, diagnostic actions, failure modes, and useful evidence from the current case can influence future skill selection, experience routing, and evidence organization rather than remaining locked inside a single execution record.

However, write-back is deliberately bounded. It occurs outside the backbone and does not modify Qwen’s weights. Moreover, write-back is not enabled in all run modes. During formal frozen evaluation, online write-back is disabled and split state is fixed, precisely to prevent state contamination from earlier evaluated cases. In this sense, reflection and write-back provide a controlled post hoc learning interface: they support experience accumulation and cognition updates outside formal evaluation, while being strictly disabled when fairness and reproducibility are paramount.

### 3.11 Training and Optimization

The optimization target of DermAgent is not to finetune Qwen and not to retrain the diagnostic backbone end to end. Instead, optimization focuses on the frozen-backbone peripheral strategy layer, namely the components that decide which skills are invoked, which experience is prioritized, and which evidence is retained for final diagnosis. This directly matches the problem setting of the paper: under a fixed vision-language backbone, can structured, experience-driven agent reasoning outperform direct prompting?

In the current implementation, the main trainable components are the controller, the retrieval reranker/scorer, and the evidence calibrator. Controller training addresses skill selection: given initial perception, clinical metadata, experience context, and the cognition state, it learns which structured clinical reasoning actions are more valuable to activate for the current case. Retrieval reranking or scoring serves experience selection and skill candidate ordering: rather than learning a new diagnostician, it reranks candidates so that the ones most useful for downstream reasoning are more likely to influence planning. Evidence calibrator training addresses evidence organization: given skill outputs, experience summaries, risk flags, uncertainty signals, and contradiction cues, it learns how to organize them more effectively for the backbone’s final diagnosis.

Training signals come from four main sources: execution records, frozen split state, helpful/harmful signals, and downstream evaluation-oriented supervision. Execution records capture input summaries, initial perception, retrieval bundles, planner decisions, skill outputs, evidence packages, final diagnoses, and reflection summaries. Frozen split state provides controlled boundaries for data sampling and state isolation. Helpful/harmful signals arise from the reflection stage’s posterior assessment of skills, referenced experience, and evidence use. Downstream evaluation-oriented supervision uses final case-level performance, deltas against the direct baseline, and conservative subset metrics to filter and constrain updates to peripheral strategy.

DermAgent follows a staged training pipeline rather than an opaque joint optimization scheme. In the current codebase, stage 0 collects and exports training data from accumulated execution records; stage 1 focuses on controller training; stage 2 focuses on retrieval scorer/reranker training; stage 3 assembles trained checkpoints into a candidate policy and evaluates that policy under frozen protocol; and stage 4 exports stable checkpoints and manages versioning. As currently implemented, controller training, retrieval scorer/reranker training, and the staged training pipeline are already on the main path. The evidence calibrator is already integrated into the main execution path and included in trainable configuration, but its training and final frozen validation remain under ongoing iteration.

Restricting optimization to the peripheral strategy layer while keeping the backbone fixed has several advantages. It improves stability, because the diagnostic behavior of the backbone remains bounded while updates are localized to intermediate strategy. It improves auditability, because any change can be mapped to a controller, retrieval, or evidence component rather than to opaque weight drift. It improves attribution, because performance changes can be analyzed in terms of changed skill selection, changed experience ranking, or changed evidence calibration. Finally, it is especially well suited to same-backbone comparison, because the difference between the baseline and DermAgent remains centered on whether structured reasoning is enabled and how its peripheral components are optimized.

### 3.12 Frozen Evaluation Setting

To evaluate the true contribution of structured agent reasoning relative to direct prompting, DermAgent adopts a frozen and fair evaluation setting. During formal evaluation, online write-back is disabled and the system is not allowed to write reflections from the current case back into the layered experience bank or cognition state. In addition, split state is fixed, meaning that the experience layer, cognition state, and relevant policy state all come from predefined split-specific snapshots rather than evolving during the evaluation run. Beyond this, the baseline and the agent share the same backbone and the same case list, and are evaluated under matched input conditions, case ordering, and protocol.

The purpose of this design is to attribute performance differences as much as possible to whether structured agent reasoning is used, rather than to differences in backbone, case list, online state contamination, or hidden extra training. DermAgent is therefore designed not only around skill-experience-cognition organization, but also around repeatable same-backbone comparison under matched case lists and matched protocol.

## 4 Experimental Setup

### 4.1 Datasets

The current experimental framework is built around dermatology image diagnosis, with inputs consisting of lesion images and optional clinical metadata. Based on the existing code and fixed-split configuration, the main confirmed data source is `pad_ufes_20`, whose default fixed split is defined as `pad_ufes_20_contiguous_v1` and automatically generates deterministic train/val/test case lists. For this built-in split, the code explicitly specifies a 70%/15%/15% partition with contiguous slicing by metadata index, ensuring compatibility with case-offset/limit-based frozen evaluation and deterministic reproducibility. At present, this is the clearest and most verifiable primary experimental split in the repository.

In addition to this main dataset, the codebase also supports running the same evaluation protocol over other dermatology data roots. ### 4.2 Label Space

The current code implements an explicit canonical dermatology label space with six normalized labels: `BCC`, `ACK`, `NEV`, `SEK`, `SCC`, and `MEL`. A unified label normalization layer maps synonyms, full disease names, and common variants onto these canonical labels. This mapping is used in case-level result alignment, top-1/top-k statistics, and error analysis, and should therefore be used consistently in formal reporting.

The malignant definition is also explicit in the current code: `BCC`, `ACK`, `SCC`, and `MEL` are treated as malignant or clinically high-risk labels, whereas `NEV` and `SEK` are treated as benign labels. This definition is used for malignant recall and safety-oriented analysis in the current project, but should not be over-interpreted as a universal oncologic convention across all external datasets or all clinical settings. When external datasets use different label spaces or different granularity, malignant-versus-benign mapping should be treated as a protocol-level approximation rather than a universally transferable fact.

For out-of-distribution external evaluation, label-space consistency is a major constraint. If an external dataset cannot be aligned one-to-one with the internal canonical label space, the evaluation should not be presented as a full six-way diagnostic benchmark. Instead, it should be restricted to malignant-versus-benign evaluation, partial label mapping, or constrained subset evaluation, depending on what alignment is trustworthy.

### 4.3 Data Split and Reproducibility

To ensure reproducibility, DermAgent uses deterministic fixed splits rather than random on-the-fly partitioning. The main split configuration is stored in code with an explicit split ID, metadata path, ratios, and slicing strategy, and can be exported as a fixed JSON file for shared use across training, validation, and testing. During formal evaluation, actual case selection is determined by split name, case offset, and limit, and the corresponding case indices, case IDs, split ID, and split JSON path are all recorded in the evaluation manifest. Repeated runs under the same configuration should therefore yield the same case list and ordering.

State is also managed in a split-aware way. Training, validation, and testing can each be paired with distinct experience roots, cognition state files, and policy snapshots, reducing leakage across splits. Reproducibility in this setting comes not only from fixed case lists, but also from fixed state partitions and frozen snapshots.

### 4.4 Backbone and Serving Setup

The main comparison in this paper is conducted under the same-backbone setting. The primary experimental path uses Qwen as the backbone through a unified serving interface that supports both initial visual understanding and final diagnosis. In the DermAgent path, Qwen first produces initial perception and diagnostic candidates from the raw inputs, and later produces the final diagnosis after receiving the evidence package. In the direct baseline, the same backbone/service directly produces the final output from the raw input. Accordingly, the backbone runtime configuration, service endpoint, model context, and prompt stack should all be recorded in the evaluation manifest.

The codebase also contains scripts and directories for alternative serving backends, such as MedGemma-related launch scripts and state roots. However, under the main research question of this paper, such cross-backbone comparisons should not replace the direct same-backbone baseline. If later versions of the paper include multiple serving backends, they should be framed as additional exploratory analyses rather than as substitutes for the main controlled baseline.

### 4.5 Baselines

The main baseline in this work is the direct Qwen baseline. Under this baseline, the backbone/service, input conditions, and case list are exactly matched to DermAgent, but no layered experience retrieval, skill selection, evidence package construction, or reflection/write-back is used. Qwen directly outputs the final diagnosis. This baseline is tightly aligned with the central research question, because it asks whether enabling structured agent reasoning improves diagnosis under a fixed backbone.

On top of this main baseline, the protocol supports several matched ablations as internal controls, including removing experience retrieval, removing skill retrieval, removing cognition bias, restricting the skill set, or removing uncertainty/escalation-related layers. These settings are not independent baselines, but tools for decomposing the contribution of the full agent.

### 4.6 Evaluation Protocol

The evaluation protocol for DermAgent is built around the principle that the question is not whether any arbitrary agent can outperform any arbitrary baseline, but whether structured, experience-driven agent reasoning can outperform direct prompting under the same backbone. The goal of the protocol is therefore not simply to produce numbers, but to ensure that all comparisons remain attributable, reproducible, and as fair as possible.

We adopt a same-backbone comparison principle. For the main comparison, the baseline is defined as direct Qwen with the same backbone/service. That is, when the agent is disabled, the exact same backbone runtime, service configuration, and input conditions are used to generate the final answer directly from the image and available metadata. The DermAgent variant uses the same backbone/service but adds the intermediate reasoning layer, including experience retrieval, skill selection, evidence organization, and evidence package construction. In this way, differences between agent and baseline are constrained to the structured reasoning scaffold rather than confounded with changes in backbone or service.

Formal comparison is conducted under frozen and fair evaluation. This means, first, that split state is fixed: the layered experience bank, cognition state, and associated policy state come from split-specific frozen snapshots rather than being accumulated during evaluation. Second, online write-back is not allowed during formal comparison. This prevents earlier evaluated cases from altering the state available to later cases.

All formal agent-versus-baseline comparisons are performed as matched-case comparisons. The agent and the baseline use exactly the same case list, the same case order, the same data split, and the same input visibility conditions. This allows case-level deltas to be aligned directly in execution records, enabling fine-grained analysis of where the agent improves, preserves, or degrades performance.

## 6 Discussion

The main contribution of DermAgent is not merely the addition of several intermediate modules, but a more explicit boundary around the role of medical agents in diagnostic tasks. The framework preserves a single final diagnostician, namely the backbone, while assigning the agent only the roles of structured evidence enhancement and intermediate reasoning organization. This constitutes a safety boundary. In high-risk, high-confusion domains such as dermatology, if peripheral modules also possess independent diagnostic authority and the final output is decided by voting, weighting, or overrides, it becomes difficult to answer which part of the system is truly responsible for the diagnosis and difficult to attribute errors to the backbone or to the intermediate modules. By contrast, the single-final-diagnostician design keeps final responsibility on the backbone side and limits the value of the agent to evidence production, evidence prioritization, and evidence organization. This makes the system more controllable in interpretation, auditability, and fair comparison.

This boundary also explains why skills are defined as structured clinical reasoning actions rather than disease classifiers. If skills were disease-specific peripheral classifiers, the system would quickly drift toward a “multiple modules guessing diseases” architecture, creating overlap between the intermediate layer and the backbone. More importantly, disease-specific sub-classifiers often degenerate into rigid heuristic attachments: they may help for some labels, but they fail to preserve the broader semantics of clinical action. In contrast, defining skills as structured clinical reasoning actions makes them correspond more naturally to real diagnostic behavior, such as observation, comparison, exclusion, risk assessment, and uncertainty auditing. This shifts the optimization target from disease outputs to the quality of intermediate reasoning actions, making planner behavior, reflection, and downstream training easier to interpret through help/harm signals, evidence utility, and routing quality.

Another central design choice is the separation between the layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer. This matters not only for software architecture, but for the semantics of continued evolution. The experience layer represents knowledge content: concrete cases, local tactics, and abstract regularities retained from history. The cognition state represents strategic state: preferences, confusion priors, and behavioral statistics formed across cases. The policy layer then separates trainable, versioned, and rollback-capable control parameters from both content and strategy memory. Without such separation, all long-term change would be mixed into a single memory pool, making it difficult to attribute errors or to perform formal evaluation. With this separation, knowledge growth, strategy drift, and parameter updates can be recorded, frozen, and compared independently, which is essential for controlled agent evolution.

In our view, same-backbone comparison under frozen and fair evaluation is especially important for agent papers. In many model papers, changing the backbone or increasing training data is itself the main source of innovation. But in a system like DermAgent, whose core object of study is intermediate reasoning structure, any comparison that simultaneously changes the backbone, the case list, the online state, or the evaluation protocol makes the resulting gain difficult to attribute. For this reason, we treat fairness, state isolation, disabled write-back, and matched-case comparison as part of the method definition rather than as auxiliary evaluation tricks. Only when the direct baseline and the full agent share the same backbone/service, the same case list, the same split state, and no online write-back during formal evaluation can one rigorously ask whether structured agent reasoning itself is useful.

Dermatology is also particularly well suited to structured agent reasoning. The task depends heavily on visible phenotype, yet visible phenotype rarely maps directly and unambiguously to final diagnosis. Instead, diagnosis is often narrowed through differential comparison, local exclusion, temporal interpretation, and risk trade-offs. Dermatology also contains several clinically meaningful high-confusion pairs and high-risk subsets, which provide strong motivation for dynamic skill selection, specialist auditing, uncertainty handling, and experience reuse. In addition, the task naturally combines images, metadata, and partial history, allowing the agent to organize evidence in a genuinely multimodal setting rather than within a purely textual question-answering environment. Dermatology is thus both complex enough to expose the limitations of direct prompting and structured enough to serve as a useful testbed for layered experience, reasoning actions, and evidence packaging.

At the same time, the current evidence remains limited in several important ways. First, the complete empirical picture is still being validated. The codebase already provides frozen evaluation, matched ablations, peripheral training pipelines, and exportable result artifacts, but that does not mean all major conclusions have been firmly established. Second, while the learned controller and evidence calibrator are already integrated into the main path and configuration system, their training stability, generalization boundary, and gains across different case distributions still require further verification. Third, external datasets do not always align with the internal canonical dermatology label space, so external validation often has to be interpreted under malignant-versus-benign, partial mapping, or subset-only conditions rather than as full multi-class generalization. Fourth, there is an inherent tension between the potential benefits of online write-back and the need for frozen formal evaluation. Allowing the system to keep updating memory and cognition during evaluation may better reflect the idea of a continuously improving agent, but it also weakens fairness and attribution. Balancing long-horizon adaptive benefit against strict frozen comparison remains an open question.

Another issue that must be confronted directly is the balance between system complexity and actual gain. DermAgent explicitly introduces a skill bank, layered experience, cognition state, policy versioning, an evidence package, and reflection/write-back. This yields a more structured and auditable system, but also increases implementation complexity, engineering cost, and experimental scope. In medical AI, a more complex system is not automatically a better one. The additional complexity is justified only if it translates into more stable error control, clearer behavioral attribution, or reproducible gains under the same backbone. Future work should therefore ask not only how to improve performance, but also which components remain necessary under different resource constraints, case distributions, and risk preferences, and which ones can be simplified.

Several future directions follow naturally from the current code and experimental framework. First, the training and evaluation of the learned controller, retrieval reranker, and evidence calibrator can be made more stable and systematically analyzed under fixed split states and candidate-policy gates. Second, the current abstract-experience and composite-skill proposal machinery can be developed into more stable reusable multi-step reasoning templates, while still respecting the final-diagnostician boundary. Third, clearer malignant-versus-benign and partial-label evaluation protocols can be developed on external dermatology datasets with alignable labels. Fourth, the field may benefit from explicitly separating two write-back regimes: one for frozen formal evaluation, prioritizing fairness and reproducibility, and another for longer-horizon online adaptation, prioritizing controlled cognitive evolution. Finally, as more execution records, paper exports, and training snapshots accumulate, DermAgent may support more rigorous case-level attribution analysis, moving from post hoc narratives to a more systematic explanation framework for why agent reasoning helps.

Overall, DermAgent is not presented as a fully converged final system. Rather, it represents a methodological stance for multimodal medical diagnosis: under a fixed backbone, one can organize a skill bank, layered experience bank, cognition state, and parameterized policy layer into an evolvable, auditable, trainable, and fairly evaluable reasoning exoskeleton. The value of this stance lies not only in whether it ultimately yields higher scores, but in whether it provides a clearer framework for studying how medical agents can learn within safety boundaries, how they should be evaluated rigorously, and how intermediate reasoning can be made explicit.

## 7 Conclusion

We present and implement DermAgent, a structured agent reasoning framework for multimodal dermatology diagnosis. Unlike approaches that improve performance by retraining the backbone or introducing parallel diagnosticians, DermAgent preserves the final diagnostic role of the dermatology vision-language backbone and organizes a skill bank, layered experience bank, cognition state, and trainable parameterized policy layer into a pre-diagnostic structured evidence scaffold. The central argument of the paper is that, under a fixed backbone, making intermediate reasoning actions, layered experience, cross-case strategic state, and evidence organization explicit provides a promising direction for medical agents that is more auditable, evolvable, and fairly evaluable.

At the current stage, the paper emphasizes system design, implementation boundaries, and evaluation discipline rather than claiming conclusive performance advantages. We argue that same-backbone comparison, frozen and fair evaluation, and matched-case analysis are especially important for distinguishing whether structured peripheral reasoning is genuinely helpful, as opposed to the system simply benefiting from a stronger model or an unfair state advantage. Further work is still needed to complete frozen comparisons, stabilize the training of the learned controller and evidence calibration modules, and extend external validation. Even so, we believe that building a structured reasoning scaffold around a fixed dermatology backbone already defines a meaningful methodological direction for medical agent research.
